The hundred-year company: what it takes to build wealth that is handed down

Adaeze Okafor, FounderOct 202610 min read

My grandmother ran a fabric shop for forty-one years. When she retired, it closed. The building was sold, the customers scattered, and what she built over a lifetime was gone in a season. Nothing was handed down except the stories.

That shop is the reason Black Inheritance exists. In Black communities, the business is often the family's main asset, and too often it ends with its founder. We set out to change that ending.

Ownership is the point

Income pays for a life. Ownership pays for the lives that come after. Every decision we make starts with one question: does this keep more ownership in the hands of the people who created the value?

“The most important deal is the one you never have to make again.”

Patience is a strategy

Private equity funds must sell within a few years. We do not. That lets us invest in things that pay back slowly: training, new plants, brands built with care. Our returns compound because we never interrupt them.

Share what you build

Every employee in our group owns shares. Founders keep stakes. Communities see their landlords, lenders and employers stay put. An inheritance means little if only a few people receive it.

We are ten years into a hundred-year project. Our job is to make sure that, this time, something is handed down.
